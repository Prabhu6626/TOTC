Manufacturing

Digitizing a Manufacturing Floor: Where to Start and What to Expect

Most manufacturing companies know they need to digitize, but the sheer scope makes it hard to begin. This guide breaks the process into practical, manageable steps—starting with the areas that deliver the fastest return.

Start with what hurts most

Don't try to digitize everything at once. Identify the one or two processes that cause the most pain—whether it's order tracking, quality reporting, or production scheduling—and start there. A focused first project delivers results quickly and builds confidence for what comes next.

Map your current process first

Before building anything, document how the process works today, including all the workarounds and informal steps that have evolved over time. This baseline ensures the new system actually fits how your team operates, not how you think they operate.

Expect a transition period

Your team has been doing things a certain way for years. New software requires new habits, and that takes time. Plan for a transition period where both old and new systems run in parallel, and invest in hands-on training—not just manuals.

Plan for data migration early

Your historical data has value. Plan how you'll move it from spreadsheets, paper files, and legacy systems into the new platform. Clean it before you migrate—bad data in a new system is still bad data, just harder to find.

Don't forget ongoing support

Launching a system is not the finish line. You'll need monitoring, patching, user support, and continuous improvement. Factor this into your budget and timeline from the start.

Real-Time Logistics Tracking: Beyond the GPS Dot on a Map

Fleet tracking technology has matured far beyond simple location pings. Modern logistics tracking connects vehicle data, load information, delivery status, and customer communication into one system.

Location is just the beginning

Knowing where a truck is matters, but knowing what it's carrying, when it will arrive, and whether the delivery window will be met matters more. Modern tracking systems connect GPS data with order information, traffic predictions, and customer portals.

Proactive vs. reactive tracking

The difference between good and great logistics technology is when you find out about problems. Reactive systems tell you a delivery is late after the fact. Proactive systems alert you when a delay is likely, giving you time to reroute, notify customers, or adjust schedules.

Driver and vehicle telemetry

Connected vehicles can report fuel consumption, engine health, driving patterns, and maintenance needs. This data helps optimize routes, reduce fuel costs, schedule preventive maintenance, and improve safety.

Customer-facing visibility

Your customers expect to track their shipments. A branded tracking portal with real-time updates, estimated arrival times, and delivery confirmations reduces "where's my order?" calls and improves customer satisfaction.

What Good IT Support Actually Looks Like

Many businesses have been burned by IT support that overpromises and underdelivers. Here's how to evaluate IT support providers and what to look for in a service agreement.

Defined scope beats "unlimited" support

Beware of providers who promise "unlimited" support. In practice, this usually means slow response times and minimal effort. A clearly defined scope with specific response targets is more honest and more useful. You know what you're getting, and the provider knows what they need to deliver.

Ask about backup testing

Every provider says they do backups. Few regularly test restores. The question isn't "do you back up our data?" but "when did you last successfully restore from a backup, and how long did it take?" If they can't answer that, your backups might not work when you need them.

Monthly reporting should be standard

Good IT support providers proactively report on system health, incidents, patches applied, and upcoming concerns. If you have to chase your provider for information about your own systems, that's a red flag.

Security is not optional

Your IT support provider should be applying security patches regularly, enforcing strong authentication, monitoring for unusual activity, and maintaining an incident response plan. If security is an "add-on" rather than a core part of their service, look elsewhere.

Building a Digital Transformation Roadmap That Actually Works

Digital transformation is a journey, not a project. A practical roadmap keeps you focused, prevents scope creep, and ensures each phase builds on the last.

Start with business outcomes, not technology

The most common mistake in digital transformation is starting with technology choices. "We need AI" or "let's move to the cloud" are technology decisions, not business outcomes. Start by defining what success looks like: faster order processing, fewer errors, better visibility, lower costs. Then work backward to the technology that gets you there.

Phase your rollout

Trying to transform everything at once overwhelms your team and your budget. Break the work into phases of 3-6 months each, with clear deliverables and measurable outcomes for each phase. Each phase should deliver standalone value—not just set up the next one.

Budget for change management

Technology is only half the equation. Your people need to adopt new tools and new ways of working. Budget time and money for training, documentation, and support during the transition. The best software in the world is useless if your team won't or can't use it.

Measure and adjust

After each phase, measure the results against your original goals. What worked? What didn't? What did you learn? Use these insights to refine the next phase. A good roadmap evolves as you learn more about what works for your organization.
